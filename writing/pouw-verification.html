Proof of useful work for verifying AI treaties

October 2026

Verifying an international agreement pausing development of AI needs evidence of how compute is used. Cryptography can give protocols that work in software, without relying on installing trusted hardware on the prover side. This post is a literature review of one such tool, proof of useful work (PoUW), with which a GPU proves that it did a given amount of work, here typically matrix multiplication. In contrast with inference verification, PoUW has been developed almost exclusively in relation to cryptocurrencies in order to use a more useful computation instead of the useless hashing used in Bitcoin. PoUW could be used to show remotely that chips are kept busy on useless tasks during an emergency shutdown, or, together with a zero-knowledge proof of the workload, that they run only declared inference. I describe the two existing constructions, by Komargodski and Weinstein for integer matrices, and by Pearl Research for the FP8 format used in AI, verifiable for now only on NVIDIA chips. Both rest on unproven hardness assumptions (the one for Pearl stated only informally), and neither means much without a trusted bound on peak chip capacity. Aside from the latter, most of the open problems are primarily mathematical and should be amenable to AI-assisted research.

Cryptography for verification

An agreement that pauses AI development, or paces the frontier, needs evidence about how declared compute is used. James Petrie's forthcoming agenda, Research Priorities for AI Compute Verification, lists many kinds of evidence. Here, I only mention three that rest on cryptography, more specifically on zero-knowledge (ZK) proofs. These protocols could run on the chips already installed as they do not depend on special hardware components used for telemetry, secure enclaves or verifier devices in the datacenter. If such protocols were formally verified and trusted, the first phase of a pause could keep the chips in use instead of decommissioning or retrofitting them.

  1. Inference verification. Show that compute runs declared inference and nothing else. Closest to deployable in the regime "verified inference vs everything else", through network certifiers with recomputation, or zero-knowledge proofs of inference (Petrie's agenda; startup Attestable reports production-scale ZK proofs).
  2. Training verification. Show what exactly happened during a training run. At the robustness a treaty needs, this is a long shot due to the large compute overhead of current methods, but Peigné-Lefebvre, Nguyen and Wang argue that the overhead is not fundamental.
  3. Proof of useful work. Show remotely that compute was occupied, with no verifier hardware on site.

The boundary between inference verification and PoUW is blurry, since both start from the observation in Petrie's agenda that "an alternative to hardware measurements is to provide the results of computations themselves as evidence of how compute is being used." The difference is that inference verification shows what was computed and says how much work this took only indirectly, under the assumption that the declared outputs could not have been produced more cheaply. Because the prover chooses the inputs, this assumption fails for easy instances, for example repeated prompts or cached prefixes. PoUW gives the lower bound on work directly. Noise derived from a public seed prevents the prover from choosing easy instances, so the results themselves certify how much work they required. On the other hand, the current PoUW schemes cover only matrix multiplication, whereas inference verification also has to deal with the nonlinear layers. These layers (softmax, GELU, normalization) are not polynomial operations, so a ZK proof cannot express them directly; provers check them against precomputed tables using lookup arguments.

One key limitation of PoUW schemes is that they do depend on knowledge of the prover's hardware, because they give only a lower bound on the work performed. Without a trusted bound on the peak capacity of the chips, a prover could run provable work on part of the capacity and use the rest for something else. Petrie proposes to obtain such a bound partly by physically examining sampled chips of each type and partly from analog sensors that monitor the chips during operation to account for overclocking. Even with such a bound, a second gap remains. LLM inference uses the matmul circuits at only about 40 to 90% of peak, so a prover whose declared workload seems to saturate the hardware may in fact have headroom. Overhead can be used as a feature to close it (h/t Paul Wang). Provable useless work running alongside the declared workload can saturate compute and memory bandwidth, leaving no room for a covert workload, at the price of throughput for the declared workload, which may be politically harder to accept.

How tight does the bound have to be? Petrie puts the question as whether the certified work represents 20% or 98% of the hardware's true capacity, and for a large prover even 98% may not be enough. A slack of 5% on a fleet of 100,000 H100s is the equivalent of 5,000 chips, which in a year of training at typical efficiency deliver about \(6 \cdot 10^{25}\) FLOP. That is close to the \(10^{26}\) FLOP reporting threshold of the former US executive order on AI. A treaty may set its threshold elsewhere, but the comparison with frontier runs is no more reassuring: Epoch AI estimates the training compute of GPT-6 Astra at \(10^{27}\) FLOP, so a single year of slack is a few percent of a frontier run. The certified work then has to account for at least 90% of the true capacity, and for the largest provers probably 98% or more (h/t Paul Wang). The uncertified capacity is the difference between the peak and the certified work, so the accuracy of the whole scheme is limited by how well the peak is known. An uncertainty of 5% in the peak uses up the margin by itself. For the largest provers, PoUW alone therefore cannot settle the question.

The arithmetic overstates the risk in one respect. Slack spread thinly over many chips is less useful than the same amount concentrated on a few, since large training runs need terabytes per second of interconnect bandwidth, which an additional layer of network certifiers is designed to remove. What matters is then less the total slack than whether it can be gathered into a cluster large enough for a training run. This protection may not last. Low-communication training is improving quickly: Google DeepMind's Decoupled DiLoCo trained a 12-billion-parameter model across four US regions and cut the bandwidth needed across eight data centers from 198 Gbps to 0.84 Gbps. If this trend continues, dispersed slack becomes nearly as useful as concentrated slack, and the argument loses its force (h/t Paul Wang). Other evidence can also catch what the tickets miss. Rahman and Tajdari classify GPU workloads as training or not from the standard NVML counters (power, utilization, memory, clocks) sampled once per second. They report 98% accuracy on undisguised workloads, and at least 99.6% against each multi-GPU evasion strategy they tested on nodes of up to 8 GPUs, even when the classifier had not been trained on that strategy. Their hardest case is the one that matters here, covert training diluted into declared inference. It was detected 87% of the time when the strategy was new to the classifier, and only 43% of the time for diluted LoRA fine-tuning designed with knowledge of the classifier. The method also assumes telemetry that the prover cannot tamper with, which brings back the trusted hardware that PoUW is meant to avoid. On current evidence, PoUW is useful as one layer among several, and the remaining risk lies in small training runs diluted into the declared workload.

Inference and training verification already have an active research community. (Though still a very small one, the workshop on verification for international AI agreements that the Future of Life Institute convened in Berkeley in February 2026 had about 40 participants.) PoUW, by contrast, has been discussed almost exclusively in connection with cryptocurrencies; on the governance side, I am aware only of the PoUW section of Petrie's agenda and a blog post by Attestable. I present this literature review to draw attention to this aspect.

How PoUW works and what it is for

In the setting we consider, the prover controls the chips and the verifier sees only the messages the prover sends. The claim to be certified reads

during the time interval \(T\), declared chips performed at least \(W\) multiply-accumulate operations on inputs committed in advance.

PoUW borrows the lottery of classical proof of work. In Bitcoin, a miner hashes the block header again and again with a changing counter (the nonce) until the hash falls below a difficulty threshold. Since each attempt succeeds with the same small probability, a winning hash shows that, on average, a known number of attempts was made. PoUW replaces the nonce by results that the prover produces in their computation. For matrix multiplication, which a GPU carries out block by block (in tiles), these are values attached to the blocks. Every hash below the threshold counts as a ticket. Since each hashed value yields a ticket with the same small probability, tickets arrive as a Poisson process whose rate is proportional to the work done. Which values are hashed, and why computing them admits no shortcut, is what distinguishes the schemes discussed below.

Tickets give a lower bound on work, and the relevant attack is therefore to produce tickets without doing the corresponding work, which would free capacity for a covert workload.

Petrie's agenda describes two uses for this. They are best treated as separate objectives, neutralizing compute and bounding the work of declared inference, even though their technical tools overlap.

  • Verifiable emergency shutdown. The chips are kept provably busy with useless work, i.e. proof of work on inputs derived from a public seed. Matrix multiplication is a better choice than hashing because that is what GPUs or TPUs are optimized for. Unlike for Bitcoin, specialized hardware (ASICs) is unlikely to produce tickets faster. This protocol can work without access to the datacenter, but removes the option of simply checking that it is idle, e.g. by satellite imagery.
  • Declared inference only. The basic evidence here comes from inference verification, i.e. a proof that the declared model produced the declared outputs. (A zero-knowledge proof keeps the inputs, especially the weights, secret.) However, this does not guarantee that nothing else was computed. PoUW supplies this complementary information. Tickets drawn from the committed matrices show how much of the chips' capacity the declared workload occupied, so that, given the peak-capacity bound, little is left for a covert workload. Attestable's blog post proposes exactly this combination. The underlying objective is to know how much work the declared inference requires. PoUW is one tool for this; protocol changes that restrict optimizations such as caching and batching are another.

Neither use removes the need for the peak-capacity bound discussed above, which PoUW cannot provide.

Why useful work is harder than useless work

Hash-based proof of work is conceptually simple. In the random-oracle model, the only way to find a hash below the threshold is to keep trying inputs. This works by definition from random input, and the main drawback is that the work serves no other purpose than to verify the work was done.

What makes PoUW harder is that we need to allow the miner to choose the inputs. The miner may exploit this by choosing easy ones; multiplying two zero matrices, for instance, requires no work at all. A PoUW scheme must therefore ensure that no choice of inputs makes tickets cheaper than the prescribed work, while keeping the honest miner's overhead negligible at \(1 + o(1)\). The matrix schemes perturb the miner's inputs with noise derived from a public seed, which also guarantees freshness, so that nothing can be precomputed. Allowing a constant-factor overhead would make the problem trivial, since one could simply solve the useful instance and run an ordinary hash-based proof of work on top of it.

The first formal definition ran into roughly this problem. Ball, Rosen, Sabin and Vasudevan introduced proofs of useful work in 2017 (ePrint 2017/203), based on the fine-grained hardness of problems such as orthogonal vectors, 3SUM and all-pairs shortest paths. However, the reviewers pointed out that the definition "is already satisfied by a generic naive construction", and the updated version, Proofs of Work from Worst-Case Assumptions (CRYPTO 2018), no longer claims usefulness. As Komargodski and Weinstein explain, the 2017 scheme required the prover to solve polylogarithmically many instances of the problem, of which only one was useful to the prover.

The idea of committing to intermediate states is older than PoUW. Monrose, Wyckoff and Rubin (NDSS 1999) let volunteer-computing workers commit to execution traces, which the verifier spot-checked by re-executing randomly chosen segments. Proofs of sequential work (Mahmoody, Moran, Vadhan 2013) and Proof-of-Learning (Jia et al. 2021, spoofed the following year) follow the same pattern. Lotteries tied to useful computation also predate the matrix schemes. Ofelimos (Fitzi, Kiayias, Panagiotakos, Russell, CRYPTO 2022) seeds each step of a local search with a hash and hashes its result, so that "the computation cannot be cut short", and comes with a full security analysis. Shoker's Proof of eXercise (IEEE NCA 2017) already observed that multiplying by the identity matrix is trivial and proposed checking the inputs before an exercise is accepted, an early version of the statistical checks in the Pearl scheme discussed below. Compared with this earlier work, the contribution of Komargodski and Weinstein is to draw the tickets from intermediate values, and to use cheaply removable noise that forces the prover to do nontrivial work even for trivial instances.

Komargodski–Weinstein: hiding the instance behind low-rank noise

The integer construction appears in Komargodski and Weinstein, Proofs of Useful Work from Arbitrary Matrix Multiplication (v4; ePrint 2025/685; versions 1 to 3 also list Itamar Schen). I abbreviate it as KW.

Suppose the miner wants to compute \(AB\) for \(n \times n\) integer matrices of its choice. After committing to them, it derives two noise matrices of rank \(r\) from the seed, \(E = E_L E_R\) and \(F = F_L F_R\), and multiplies the noised matrices \(A' = A + E\) and \(B' = B + F\). The useful product is then recovered by subtracting the correction

\[AB = A'B' - \big(AF + E(B + F)\big).\]

Calculating the correction requires \(O(n^2 r)\) operations, which for \(r \ll n\) means \(1 + o(1)\) overhead.

The output alone cannot serve as the proof of work, since for \(A = B = 0\) the noised product is \(EF\), which can be computed in \(O(n^2 r)\). KW therefore propose to hash the transcript of the computation, i.e. all the \(r \times r\) block partial sums \(C^{(\ell)}_{ij}\) of the multiplication algorithm. Their Assumption 6.4 states that computing all these intermediate values for a product of random rank-\(r\) matrices takes time \(\Omega(n^{\omega_r + 1}/r)\), where \(\omega_r\) is the exponent of rectangular matrix multiplication.

The bound \(n^{\omega_r+1}/r\) is what fast matrix multiplication, the line of algorithms started by Strassen, achieves on the transcript; the assumption claims only that nothing does better. Asymptotically this beats the honest miner as it costs \(n^{2.7+o(1)}\) for \(r = n^{0.3}\). In practice the gap is a small constant, because the asymptotically fastest algorithms are galactic or memory-heavy, and Strassen's algorithm applied to each \(r \times r\) block saves only a factor \(r^{3-\log_2 7}\) in operation count, about 2 at \(r = 32\), which tensor cores cannot exploit.1 For a treaty, this is a constant factor in the work bound, comparable to the uncertainty about peak capacity of the chips. KW leave open whether a PoUW with a Strassen-like baseline exists.

The paper states three open problems: tasks beyond matrix multiplication (Problem 3.1), better matrix-multiplication PoUW with lower overhead (Problem 3.2), and, new in v4, PoUW from standard, well-studied assumptions (Problem 3.3).

Assumption 6.4 concerns a single instance, whereas a treaty faces a stream of challenges over time. KW model the stream as a Poisson process in an appendix, but its security still has to be derived from the single-instance assumption (problem 2 below). The closest precedent is Ball et al. (2018), who proved non-amortizability for a different problem.

Version 4 of the paper adds a second scheme based on rotations that avoids denoising. The inputs are rotated as \(A' = AR\) and \(B' = R^\top B\) with \(R = H_n D\), where \(H_n\) is a Hadamard matrix and \(D\) a random block-diagonal matrix with \(\pm 1\) entries and block size \(d\). The rotation is meant to cancel in the final output, \(A'B' = AB\),2 and the rotation itself costs \(O(n^2 \log n)\). This works because in the partial sums \(\sum_k (AR)_{ik} (R^\top B)_{kj}\) the rotations do not cancel. The scheme requires \(A\) and \(B\) to have high rank, and the paper leaves open how to ensure or test this. Its Conjecture A.1 states that for full-rank \(A\), \(B\), \(r = \sqrt n\) and \(d = O(1)\), computing all \(n^3/r^3\) partial sums has amortized cost \(\Omega(r^3)\) per partial sum in the word-RAM model, even after \(O(n^3)\) preprocessing. As heuristic support, KW note that no known algorithm multiplies an arbitrary \(r \times r\) submatrix of the FFT matrix by a vector in \(o(r^2)\) time without massive preprocessing. The conjecture thus asks why the global structure of the Hadamard matrix does not help on its local blocks.

For the treaty setting, the most useful idea of KW is the low-rank noise: it is derived from a seed, cheap to remove, and takes away the prover's freedom to choose easy instances. The transcript is less convenient. With secret weights its tickets have to be verified in zero knowledge, and combining them with a proof of the workload is not straightforward (see Deployment in a treaty). Moreover, KW work with integers, while inference runs in floating point. Pearl, the blockchain project discussed next, keeps the noise but drops the transcript.

Pearl: quantization removes the shortcut but needs a new assumption

Shortly after the paper appeared, its authors founded Pearl Research, which runs a blockchain on a modified version of their protocol. In September 2026 the company published the Pearl Floating Point Scheme Specification. Its unit of work is FP8 (E4M3) matrix multiplication on whitelisted GPUs.

The miner first adds the noise and then quantizes,

\[\tilde C = Q(A + N_A)\, Q(B + N_B)^\top,\]

where \(Q\) is entrywise quantization to FP8 and \(N_A\), \(N_B\) are seed-derived noise matrices of rank \(r\). The useful output \(\hat C = \tilde C - A N_B^\top - N_A B^\top - N_A N_B^\top\) is accurate to FP8 precision. Since \(Q\) is nonlinear, \(Q(N_A)\) is in general not of low rank, and the shortcut on the output disappears. Tickets can therefore hash the output tiles directly, and the verifier recomputes a winning tile bit for bit.

Security rests on Assumption 1: with high probability over random \(r\)-dimensional subspaces, computing \(Q(N_A) Q(N_B)^\top\) with rows drawn from these subspaces is "not substantially easier" than a generic quantized product \(Q(A)Q(B)\) of the same size.

As stated, the informal assumption comes with no explicit bounds due to vagueness in "not substantially easier" and "on average". The whitepaper gives neither a reduction nor evidence beyond the design reasoning, and I am not aware of any published cryptanalysis, though no attack is known either. The assumption also concerns only \(A = B = 0\). For useless work on seed-derived inputs, as in an emergency shutdown, the jackpot policy is not needed. Security against inputs with planted structure rests on a "jackpot policy" of statistical checks on the miner's inputs (entry liveness, a noise floor, tamed products, unpredictable summands) with heuristically chosen constants, and the whitepaper lists seed grinding among the known speedups.

Bit-exact recomputation requires an exact model of each device's matrix arithmetic, including rounding, subnormal numbers, accumulator width, and the grouping and order of summation. For NVIDIA GPUs, such a model is Hawkeye (Badash, Boneh, Komargodski, Srivastava, MLSys 2026), a joint work of Pearl Research and Stanford, which reproduces Tensor Core products on a CPU using several black-box tests. It covers Ampere, Hopper and Ada Lovelace (the last follows the same model as Ampere) in FP16, BF16 and FP8, with bit-exact agreement on 100,000 random \(16 \times 16\) tiles; FP8 results are reported for Hopper only. The whitepaper adds parameters for Blackwell. As far as I know, no similar characterization is available for TPUs, Trainium or Maia, since vendors document formats (for instance FP8 inputs with FP32 accumulation) but not the bit-level details of accumulation. A treaty has to cover these chips as well, so every new generation would require either vendor cooperation or reverse engineering in the style of Hawkeye.

The integer protocol does not have this problem, because integer matrix multiplication is bit-exact on any hardware. Basu (arXiv:2606.04819, Sec. 6) obtained pool-accepted shares for the integer protocol deployed by Pearl on NVIDIA (RTX 3090) and AMD MI300X GPUs, and ran the same miner on a CPU and on Apple Silicon. The move to FP8 thus gives up hardware portability in exchange for efficiency on NVIDIA tensor cores. It is an open question whether systolic arrays, whose accumulation order is fixed in hardware, are easier to model than GPU kernels. Inference verification by recomputation faces the same gap, since it also needs bit-exact nonlinear operations.

Pearl reports an overhead of about 4 to 5% on LLM inference (blog: +5.08% for Llama 70B, +3.9% for DeepSeek V3.2), measured on the integer scheme. Basu (arXiv:2606.04819, Sec. 4.6) estimates, without a measurement, that coupling inference with mining costs 10 to 30% of the hashrate, which suggests that the overhead is not negligible for a miner. The whitepaper also specifies an extension to mixture-of-experts models and a zero-knowledge verifier.

Basu's study shows how the deployed network behaves in practice. The dominant miner binary contains no inference code, and verification accepts random matrices, which is documented behavior of the integer protocol. This is what one should expect, because PoUW on its own certifies work and not usefulness; to certify usefulness, the tickets have to be bound to a proof of the workload, as discussed above.

Deployments and related protocols

BTX is a settlement chain running on mainnet using matrix multiplication over \(\mathbb{F}_{2^{31}-1}\) as the proof of work, with inputs derived from seeds in the block header; according to its specification, the construction is derived from KW. The computation is not doing anything useful, so it is closer to the protocol for shutdown.

Attestable's Pacing AI Requires Proof is, as far as I know, the only proposal to use PoUW for compute governance. It combines ZK proofs that the credited workload follows the policy with PoUW accounting that bounds the uncredited capacity.

Monfared et al. (arXiv:2602.09369) survey software-only methods for checking that a GPU performed a given amount of work at a given time without trusting its hardware, including memory-hard proof of work, matrix-multiplication challenges, verifiable delay functions and memory probes. All of them rely on timing. The verifier sends fresh random challenges and infers the available compute from how quickly the answers come back. Their matrix challenge is an interactive variant of useless work, in which the verifier chooses the matrices, times the answer and checks the product cheaply. Since the verifier picks the inputs, the noise and tickets of KW are not needed.

Their memory probe comes closest to the PoUW based on memory bandwidth suggested in Petrie's agenda, in which the prover hashes the data that its workload loads into cache. At random times the verifier asks for a hash over a large random dataset it has placed in GPU memory, and a slow answer shows that the data was moved off the chip. The memory is thus occupied by useless data. I am not aware of a variant that uses the workload's own memory traffic. Petrie's agenda also proposes a memory-state challenge. The verifier asks for \(y = f(x, \mathrm{nonce})\) over the claimed contents \(x\) of high-bandwidth memory, where \(f\) requires so much memory bandwidth and matrix multiplication that only a GPU can answer in time. \(f\) also uses many hashes to occupy the general-purpose compute that could otherwise decompress weights stored in compressed form.

Deployment in a treaty

Based on the sources, PoUW would fit into a verification stack as follows.

  • Prover. The Pearl miner commits to its operands with Merkle trees, derives the seeds from the commitments and a public beacon, adds noise and quantizes in \(O((m+n)kr)\) operations, runs the usual matrix-multiplication kernel on the noised operands, scans the output tiles for tickets, and finally removes the noise. This requires modified GPU software (matrix-multiplication kernels that inject the noise and hash the tiles), but no new or modified hardware.
  • Challenge channel. On a blockchain the seeds come from the chain tip, while in a treaty they would come from the verifier, either as a randomness beacon or through the network certifier or challenge device from Petrie's agenda. The latency and rate of this channel determine how finely the work can be accounted for.
  • Verifier. The verifier recomputes one winning tile bit for bit, a constant amount of work regardless of the underlying job of the prover. The list of whitelisted devices is part of the protocol.
  • Composition with ZK. The Pearl whitepaper already specifies how to verify a ticket in zero knowledge, with a public ticket and secret operands. What is missing is a link to the zero-knowledge proof of the workload. If PoUW runs separately, the tickets bound only the useless work, while the zkML proof shows what was computed but not how much work it took. The verifier then has to treat all capacity spent on inference as possibly covert, and can close this gap only by filling the chips with useless work, which leaves no room for inference. If instead the tickets are drawn from the same committed \(A\) and \(B\) that the zkML proof opens, the inference carries a work bound at \(1 + o(1)\) overhead, and since the seeds come from a beacon, the tickets also time-stamp the workload, which a zkML proof alone does not.
  • Accounting. The verifier sees only the tickets, so the evidence is statistical and accumulates over time. For an honest miner, and modeling the hash as a random oracle, each tile yields a ticket independently with the same small probability, so the number of tickets is approximately Poisson distributed with mean proportional to the work, and the work estimate after \(N\) tickets has a relative error of about \(1/\sqrt N\).

How hard this link is depends on the zkML proof system (examples are zkLLM and Kang et al.). A single ticket concerns one tile, so proving it in zero knowledge should be cheap. The difficulty is that a ticket hashes specific values, the block partial sums in KW or the output tiles in Pearl, and the workload proof has to reproduce the same values. Whether they already appear in the workload proof, or have to be committed separately, depends on how that proof handles matrix products. With open weights, as in a regime of published training runs (Kokotajlo et al., AI 2040: Plan A, AI Futures Project, 2026), no ZK proof is needed, tickets can be checked by recomputation, and the problem disappears.

Limitations of KW and their current status

Petrie's agenda lists four limitations of KW, besides the prerequisite of a capacity bound. The table shows where they stand after the sources discussed above.

Limitation Status after the 2026 sources
Unproven low-rank hardness Still open. Pearl replaces it by a different unproven assumption, stated informally.
No floating point Addressed by Pearl (FP8, bit-exact through Hawkeye), under the new assumption and a heuristic input policy, and for NVIDIA only.
Overhead untested Pearl reports about 4 to 5% on LLM inference, BTX 10 to 16.5% for its useless variant. Vendor numbers, not independent.
Attention shapes Not addressed. Pearl adds mixture-of-experts support and a minimum tile dimension \(k \ge 1024\).
Peak-capacity bound Untouched. It is a hardware question, outside PoUW.

Open problems for mathematicians

  1. Evidence for KW's Assumption 6.4 beyond the known algorithms, such as reductions or lower bounds in restricted models, or else PoUW from standard assumptions (KW's Problem 3.3).
  2. Many challenges at once. Assumption 6.4 says that a single instance is hard, but a prover in a treaty answers a long stream of challenges and may amortize work across them. Show that single-instance hardness implies that solving many instances jointly does not raise the ticket rate per unit of work (a non-amortization result, as Ball et al. (2018) proved for their problem), or else state an amortized assumption directly, as Conjecture A.1 does.
  3. The self-canceling scheme. Find evidence for Conjecture A.1, i.e. that \(r \times r\) blocks of randomized Hadamard products cannot be computed faster locally, and a cheap test, ideally a SNARK, that the committed \(A\) and \(B\) have high rank.
  4. A formal version of Assumption 1 of Pearl. A first step would be to understand the structure of entrywise-quantized random low-rank matrices (rank, spectrum, distance to low rank) and of the product of their rounding errors.
  5. Soundness of the jackpot policy. Do the statistical checks exclude every input that makes tiles predictable, and with what constants?
  6. Other workloads. PoUW for attention-shaped products and for memory-bound work, and challenges that saturate memory bandwidth and compute at the same time, so that no capacity is left for decompression or covert work.
  7. Composition. Combine PoUW tickets with a ZK proof that the committed matrices are the declared workload, as one protocol with one security statement.

Problems 3 to 5 seem the most tractable. Each concerns a fixed, explicitly specified scheme, and progress can come as a proof or as an attack that can be tested numerically.

Solving these problems does not depend on hardware, but the solutions are useful only together with two things outside their scope: a reliable declaration of the hardware with a bound on its peak capacity, and a way to determine the true cost of the declared inference, which caching, batching and other optimizations can push well below its nominal FLOP count (h/t Paul Wang). Both are bottlenecks, and ideas that solve or circumvent them are needed as much as progress on the problems above.

Outlook

PoUW would remove verifier hardware from the datacenter, which, in Petrie's words, largely removes "both the prover's and the verifier's security concerns". The integer construction already runs on blockchains, and the floating-point one is specified. Both rest on hardness assumptions that are one or two years old and, as far as I know, have been studied mainly by their authors, who also run Pearl. The move to floating point replaced one unproven assumption by another.

In my reading, this is a severely understudied area. If the problem of bounding the peak output of deployed hardware is solved, PoUW and inference verification could form the backbone of the verification protocols needed for an international treaty to pause AI development, a key component of our plan to deal with existential risks posed by AI.

Because most of the open problems here are mathematical in nature, I expect most of them to be amenable to AI-assisted research given its recent progress. I am convinced that this area, as well as verification of LLMs and other software in general, merits more attention from mathematicians and funders.

  1. AI-assisted search has improved small schemes: AlphaEvolve found a \(4 \times 4\) scheme with 48 multiplications in 2025 (a rational version is in arXiv:2506.13242). Since \(\log_4 48 \approx 2.793\) against \(\log_2 7 \approx 2.807\), this raises the gain at \(r = 32\) only from 1.95 to 2.05. The slack also matters only for KW's exact integer scheme. In the Pearl scheme, which is verified by bit-exact recomputation of FP8 tiles, a fast algorithm changes the rounding and fails verification, unless it reproduces the pinned accumulation order exactly. ↩

  2. As literally specified, the cancellation fails for block size \(d \ge 2\). Since \(H_n H_n^\top = nI\), we have \(RR^\top = H_n DD^\top H_n^\top\), which is a multiple of the identity only if every \(d \times d\) block of \(D\) is itself a Hadamard matrix, an event of probability at most \(2^{-n/d}\). The output is preserved for \(d = 1\), i.e. a random diagonal sign matrix as in the randomized Hadamard transform, after normalizing by \(1/\sqrt n\), or if the blocks of \(D\) are random orthogonal matrices, which is presumably what is intended. ↩
